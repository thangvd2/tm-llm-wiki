# Change Control Board

## [](#purpose "Copy link to heading")Purpose

The purpose of establishing a change control board is to manage and oversee changes to project scope, requirements, and deliverables throughout the project lifecycle. It serves as a governance mechanism to:

-   Evaluate and prioritise change requests based on their impact, urgency, and alignment with project objectives.
    
-   Assess the potential risks, benefits, and implications of proposed changes on project scope, schedule, budget, and quality.
    
-   Record the outcome of change control board decisions, the rationale and impact on programme outcomes.
    
-   Ensure that changes are implemented in a controlled and coordinated manner to minimise disruption and maximise value delivery.
    
-   Communicate change decisions and their implications to stakeholders to manage expectations and facilitate alignment.
    

By implementing effective change control, programmes can ensure changes are well-managed, risks are mitigated and delivery is successful and in accordance with the business case and programme objectives.

## [](#guidance "Copy link to heading")Guidance

The change control board should comprise of key stakeholders responsible for evaluating and approving change requests. Typical members include:

-   **Chairperson:** Leads meetings, sets agendas, and ensures focused discussions. This may be the Programme Director/Manager or in large programmes, there may be a dedicated Change Manager.
    
-   **Programme Sponsor:** Ensures alignment between change requests and the strategic objectives of the programme.
    
-   **Programme Manager:** Oversees project execution and provides context on change impacts.
    
-   **Product Owner:** Ensures that proposed changes are appropriately prioritised and aligned with the needs of the business and priorities of the end users.
    
-   **Technical Architect:** Offers technical expertise and evaluates the feasibility of changes from a technical perspective.
    
-   **Quality Assurance Representative:** Ensures proposed changes meet quality standards.
    
-   **Finance:** Assesses financial implications and alignment with the programme budget.
    
-   **Stakeholder Representatives:** Voices the interests and concerns of key stakeholder groups including the end users
    
-   **Risk and Compliance:** Evaluates risks associated with proposed changes and suggests mitigation strategies and ensures adherence to legal and regulatory requirements.
    
-   **Subject Matter Experts:** May be invited on an ad-hoc basis, dependent on the nature of the changes being evaluated.
    

The key components of establishing an effective change control board are:

1.  **Define the composition, roles, and responsibilities:** Identify key members of the change control board, including project managers, subject matter experts, senior stakeholders, and decision-makers. Ensure roles such as Chair, Change Request Evaluators, and Record Keeper are assigned with clear responsibilities for reviewing, assessing, and approving changes.
    
2.  **Develop a change control process:** Define the tooling and structured process for submitting, evaluating, and approving change requests and communicate clear procedures for raising changes, prioritising them, and setting decision timelines.
    
3.  **Establish criteria and thresholds:** Define the criteria for assessing change requests, focusing on the potential impact on scope, schedule, budget, quality, and risk. This includes setting thresholds to determine when changes require change control board approval versus delegated authority.
    
4.  **Conduct impact analysis and risk assessment:** Ensure each proposed change undergoes a thorough impact analysis on scope, cost, and timelines and evaluate the risks to determine potential negative effects and propose mitigation plans.
    
5.  **Hold regular meetings:** Schedule periodic CCB meetings to review, prioritise, and make decisions on pending change requests.
    
6.  **Document and communicate decisions:** Maintain detailed records of approved changes, including justifications, impacts, and decisions made and ensure these are readily accessible so all programme team members are aware.
    
7.  **Execution of change decisions:** Ensure that all approved changes are properly implemented, through assigning owners and tracking completion.
    

There are different models for execution of a change control board:

-   **Centralised:** Establish a centralised governing body responsible for overseeing change decisions across the whole programme.
    
    -   **Pros:** consistency, centralised control, and streamlined decision-making.
        
    -   **Cons:** may lead to bottlenecks and slower response times.
        
    
-   **Decentralised:** Delegate Change Control to project teams or departments, with each responsible for their own change decisions.
    
    -   **Pros:** flexibility, faster decision-making, and alignment with squad or phase specific needs.
        
    -   **Cons:** may result in inconsistency with the wider programme due to lack of centralised oversight.
        
    
-   **Virtual:** Changevcontrol decisions are made using digital tools, with members collaborating remotely.
    
    -   **Pros:** flexible, scalable and responsive which works well across multiple timezones.
        
    -   **Cons:** may result in communication gaps and delays. Requires robust tools and controls.
        
    

### [](#common_pitfalls "Copy link to heading")Common Pitfalls

-   **Uncontrolled scope creep:** When requirements expand beyond the original scope without proper approval, leading to delays, budget overruns, and reduced quality. If not managed early, it can seriously jeopardise project success.
    
-   **Undefined criteria for change evaluations:** Without clear guidance on how changes are assessed, decision-making becomes inconsistent. This raises the risk of misaligned priorities, scope creep, and delivery failure. Defined criteria help ensure changes are evaluated and controlled properly.
    
-   **Lack of transparency:** Unclear decision-making can cause confusion and mistrust among stakeholders. Transparent processes keep everyone informed about changes, decisions, and their impact on scope, budget, and timelines.
    
-   **Misalignment with organisational goals:** Ignoring the wider programme and strategic objectives results in wasted effort and diminished value. Ensuring alignment keeps changes focused on supporting broader business goals.
    
-   **Striving for perfection:** There may be many options to consider and not one perfect solution. There will sometimes be compromises and it is important to understand the balance between scope, time and cost.
    
-   **Overly bureaucratic:** Change control boards can become too process-heavy, slowing decision-making and causing delays, frustration and missed opportunities.
    
-   **Incorrect expertise/representation:** Without the right mix of knowledge and stakeholders, change requests may not be properly evaluated. A balanced board ensures informed decisions.
