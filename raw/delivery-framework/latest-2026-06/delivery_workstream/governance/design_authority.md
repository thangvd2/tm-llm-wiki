# Design Authority

## [](#purpose "Copy link to heading")Purpose

The design authority is the framework and associated governing body within the programme that is responsible for ensuring the alignment of design decisions with the overarching business strategy, architectural principles, and technical standards throughout the delivery lifecycle.

It plays a critical role in a large-scale core banking delivery programme, particularly in environments where multiple teams or vendors are contributing to the design and implementation of solutions.

The design authority ensures:

-   Design decisions across different projects, teams, and/ or components are consistent and aligned with the client organisation’s long-term strategy, goals, and architecture. This prevents siloed or fragmented solutions that might not integrate well.
    
-   Designs meet predefined standards of quality, functionality, scalability, and performance. This safeguards from suboptimal designs that could create technical debt or lead to future complications.
    
-   Risks are mitigated, such as technical misalignment, integration challenges, and potential security vulnerabilities. It can foresee and address potential issues before they become significant problems.
    
-   A clear framework for decision-making, with all design changes or proposals undergo proper scrutiny and approval. This provides accountability and helps ensure transparency and consistency in the design process.
    
-   Collaboration between technical teams, business stakeholders, and external partners. By bringing together key players involved in the design process, the Design Authority ensures that diverse perspectives are considered in design decisions.
    
-   Designs implemented today can adapt to future needs and technological advancements. It promotes flexibility, scalability, and reuse, reducing the need for costly redesigns.

## [](#guidance "Copy link to heading")Guidance

-   A design authority typically consists of senior architects, business analysts, technical leads, and relevant experts in both technical and business domains, including the partner(s) and Thought Machine. There should be clear roles assigned including a Chair and Record Keeper.
    
-   A core-banking programme of this complexity should have its own Design Authority; this may feed decisions into other design authorities across the client organisation.
    
-   Whilst it is not uncommon to have separate technology and business design authorities, Thought Machine’s experience is that these are best combined into a single programme design authority.
    
-   There are different models for execution of a design authority:
    
    1.  **Centralised**: Establish a centralised governing body responsible for overseeing design decisions across the whole programme.
        
        -   Pros: consistency, centralised control, and streamlined decision-making.
            
        -   Cons: may lead to bottlenecks and slower response times.
            
        
    2.  **Decentralised**: Delegate Design Authority to project teams or departments, with each responsible for their own design decisions.
        
        -   Pros: flexibility, faster decision-making, and alignment with squad or phase specific needs.
            
        -   Cons: may result in inconsistency with the wider programme and lack of centralised oversight.
            
        
    3.  **Virtual**: Change control decisions are made using digital tools, with members collaborating remotely.
        
        -   Pros: flexible, scalable and responsive which works well across multiple timezones.
            
        -   Cons: May result in communication gaps and delays. Requires robust tools and controls.
            
        
    
-   The design authority should operate under a set of defined architectural principles, technical standards, and business guidelines. These standards guide decision-making, ensuring that all designs adhere to agreed-upon requirements such as security, scalability, user experience, and compliance.
    
-   A core responsibility of the design authority is to establish and manage the design review process. This includes the submission of design proposals, a review of design documents, and the approval or rejection of designs. It serves as the arbiter of changes, ensuring that they are documented, justified, and aligned with the strategic objectives.
    
-   The design authority is a decision making authority. It must have the authority to enforce design standards and approve or reject proposed solutions or any changes to previously approved designs. Its decisions must be respected and implemented by project teams to maintain control over the design process.
    

### [](#common_pitfalls "Copy link to heading")Common pitfalls

-   **Overly bureaucratic:** Design authority can become too focused on process and governance, creating a slow, rigid approval system; this can lead to long delays in decision-making, causing frustration and hindering progress. Ensure that the design authority focuses on key, high impact and timely decisions.
    
-   **Lack of authority:** Ambiguity in the scope and decision-making power of the design authority leads to confusion, conflicting decisions or uncertainty about who has the final say in the decision making. Roles, responsibilities and decision-making scope of the design authority needs to be defined clearly to ensure it operates within well-established boundaries and collaborates effectively with other design authorities / governance bodies.
    
-   **Incorrect expertise / representation:** Not having the right mix of people that represent all stakeholders means that certain aspects can get overlooked in the decision making process resulting in suboptimal designs or too much of a technical focus. The design authority should comprise of cross-functional stakeholders (technical, business, architectural) and be regularly assessed to ensure there is a balance between technical rigour and alignment to programme goals.
    
-   **Resistance to change or innovation:** A rigid adherence to standards and processes can stifle innovation and not adapt to the changing needs of the business, market or technology. If the Design Authority is overly conservative or resistant to new ideas, it can lead to dated designs and prevent teams from exploring innovative or disruptive solutions. A balance is required between enforcing standards and encouraging innovation to allow for significant improvements or competitive advantage. Standards should be regularly reviewed and decisions re-evaluated in response to new information.
    
-   **Poor communication:** Ineffective communication between the design authority and the wider programme team can lead to misunderstandings, unclear expectations, and missed deadlines. Clear and open communication channels ensure that design standards, decisions and changes are well-documented, timely, understood and accessible to all.
