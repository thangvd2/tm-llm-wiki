# Status Reporting

## [](#purpose "Copy link to heading")Purpose

Status reporting is a crucial activity for programme delivery. Its primary purpose is to provide stakeholders with transparent and accurate information about the progress, risks, and challenges encountered during the implementation of these programmes, workstreams, etc.

By undertaking status reporting, delivery managers and teams:

-   **Ensure Alignment and Transparency:** Status reporting helps to align all stakeholders, including executives, project teams, sponsors, partners and clients, by providing a clear understanding of the project’s current status, objectives, and milestones. It fosters transparency, enabling stakeholders to make informed decisions and adjust strategies as needed.
    
-   **Monitor Progress and Performance:** One of the key objectives of status reporting is to monitor the progress and performance of the project against its predetermined goals and timelines. It allows project managers to identify any deviations from the original plan, assess the impact of these deviations, and take corrective actions promptly to mitigate risks and keep the project on track.
    
-   **Identify and Mitigate Risks:** Potential risks and issues that may hinder the successful delivery of the programme can be identified. By regularly reporting on these, their likelihood, and potential impacts, stakeholders can proactively implement risk mitigation strategies and contingency plans, reducing the likelihood of programme failure or delays.
    
-   **Facilitate Decision-Making:** Effective status reporting provides decision-makers with the information they need to make timely and well-informed decisions. It enables stakeholders to prioritise tasks, allocate resources efficiently, and address any emerging issues before they escalate into significant problems.
    

Outputs:

-   Regular status reports, detailing progress, achievements, risks, issues, and upcoming milestones.
    
-   Key performance indicators (KPIs) and metrics tracking project performance against goals.
    
-   Risk registers identifying and assessing potential risks and mitigation strategies.
    
-   Comprehensive RAID logs and action plans outlining steps to address any issues or concerns raised during reporting.

## [](#guidance "Copy link to heading")Guidance

-   **Define the audience and frequency**
    
    -   **Frequency:** Establish a consistent reporting cadence (e.g., weekly, bi-weekly, monthly) that aligns with the programme’s ways of working and stakeholder needs. There may be multiple levels of reporting depending on the complexity and governance structure.
        
    -   **Audience:** Tailor reports to the needs of different audiences, such as programme sponsors, steering committees, senior management, and team members, ensuring the right level of detail and focus for each group. Ensure that stakeholders have visibility into the project’s progress and challenges, to avoid misalignment, misunderstandings, and decreased confidence in the programme’s success.
        
    
-   **Use standardised templates and automate**
    
    -   Develop standardised templates for reports to maintain consistency and facilitate comparison across projects. Where possible leverage project management tooling and automate data inputs where possible to reduce the amount of manual effort. Reports typically contain the following information:
        
        -   **Executive Summary:** A brief overview of the programme’s current status, highlighting key achievements, significant risks, and any major decisions needed.
            
        -   **Milestone and Deliverable Tracking:** Update on the progress of key milestones and deliverables, including whether they are on track, at risk, or delayed.
            
        -   **Key Accomplishments:** Summarise key accomplishments since the last report to demonstrate progress and celebrate success.
            
        -   **Upcoming Activities:** Outline key activities and priorities for the next reporting period, setting clear expectations for what is coming up.
            
        -   **Metrics:** key performance indicators (KPIs) relevant to project objectives and stakeholder interests. for example. sprint burndown, team velocity, automated test coverage, number of open/closed defects, number of accounts migrated.
            
        -   **Risk and Issue Management:** A summary of the top risks and issues, including their potential impact, status, and mitigation actions being taken.
            
        -   **Budget and Resource Status:** Provide an update on the programme’s budget and resource utilisation, highlighting any variances from the plan.
            
        -   **Decisions and Action Items:** Capture key decisions made during the reporting period, any outstanding actions that need attention and who owns execution.
            
        
    

### [](#common_pitfalls "Copy link to heading")Common Pitfalls

1.  **Lack of engagement**: Reporting becomes a tick in the box exercise, there is no discussion around information on the report or action taken. Risks and issues may go unnoticed or unaddressed, increasing the likelihood of project delays, cost overruns, or failure. Reports should be used to shape governance forum agenda items, focusing on exceptions and where action is required.
    
2.  **Overloading reports with data**: Including excessive detail or irrelevant information can overwhelm stakeholders and dilute the message.
    
3.  **Lack of timeliness**: Delayed reporting or stale information can undermine the usefulness of reports. Decision-making may be hampered by a lack of accurate and timely information, resulting in attention being focused on the wrong priorities.
    
4.  **Ignoring qualitative insights**: Relying solely on quantitative data may overlook valuable qualitative insights and stakeholder perspectives. Supplement data with contextual information and analysis to help stakeholders understand the implications and make informed decisions.
    
5.  **Inconsistent reporting**: Inconsistencies in reporting format, content, or frequency can lead to confusion and misinterpretation.
    
6.  **Failure to address action items or risks**: Reporting should not only highlight issues but also track progress on action items and resolutions.
    
7.  **Relevance of metrics**: Reports tend to append rather than replace information over time. As the programme evolves through the delivery lifecycle priorities and focus shifts, efforts need to be put in place to ensure reports continue being impactful with everything contained still being relevant to the programme. Regularly ask \`why is this information being reported?'.
    
8.  **Ignoring stakeholder feedback**: Failing to solicit and incorporate stakeholder feedback can result in reports that do not meet their needs or expectations. Regularly review and refine reporting processes based on feedback and lessons learned to optimise effectiveness.
